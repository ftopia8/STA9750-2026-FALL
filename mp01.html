load_netflix_data <- function(){
  library(tidyverse)
  library(rvest)
  library(readxl)
  library(fs)
  
  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")
  
  if(!dir_exists(data_dir)){
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recurse=TRUE)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )
  
  download_if_needed <- function(url, local){
    if(!file_exists(local)){
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }
    
    return(local)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS |>
    map(read_html) |>
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |>
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |>
    enframe("period", "url") |>
    mutate(path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")),
           path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed),
           data = map(path, read_excel, skip=3, na="*"),
           data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))) |>
    unnest(data) |>
    select(-url, -path) |>
    mutate(year=str_sub(period, 1, 4),
           half=str_sub(period, start=6),
           period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 1, 7), 1),
           period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 6, 12), if_else(half==1, 30, 31))) |>
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |>
    mutate(live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"),
           clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
           Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm=TRUE)),
           .by=Title,
           Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"),
           Title = str_trim(Title)) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(year,
           period,
           period_start,
           period_end,
           Title,
           -half,
           Runtime,
           Views,
           `Hours Viewed`,
           `Release Date`,
           `Available Globally?`,
           live_event) |>
    rename(title = Title,
           runtime = Runtime,
           views = Views,
           hours_viewed = `Hours Viewed`,
           release_date = `Release Date`,
           available_globally = `Available Globally?`) |>
    mutate(year = as.integer(year),
           available_globally = available_globally == "Yes")
}

The data set has 61,892 rows and 11 columns, but only 24,427 distinct titles. This is because the same title can appear in several reporting periods, so each row is a title-period pair rather than a unique show or movie.

After cleaning, the column types look sensible. year is an integer, the period and release dates are stored as dates, title and period are text, and available_globally and live_event are logical (TRUE/FALSE). The numeric columns are runtime, views, and hours_viewed.

Missing values are the main issue. views is missing for 18,198 rows (29.4%), and runtime is missing for 13,085 rows. This limits any analysis that relies on views or on runtime-based comparisons, so I will rely mostly on hours_viewed, which has no missing values.

hours_viewed is heavily skewed. The median title has 1,800,000 hours, the mean is 8,265,858, and the maximum is 964,100,000. A small number of major hits account for a large share of viewing, while most titles have comparatively little. Some release dates also fall before the start of the reporting period, which is expected for older titles that stayed in the catalog.

Strategy Memo

To: Netflix Senior Leadership From: Farah Topia, Content Analytics Re: Where to Put the 2027 Content Budget

Summary

As I completed the most watched Netflix profect, this is my analysis and what I have determined

Seven “What We Watched” reports, covering 2023H1 through 2026H1, show where Netflix viewers actually spend their time. This memo recommends three priorities for 2027: keep globally available originals at the center of the slate, fund new seasons of proven franchises before one-off launches, and treat live events as a small number of premium bets rather than a volume play. I exclude 2023H1 from trend comparisons because that report lists far more titles than any later one and is not comparable.

1. Globally available originals carry the catalog

Globally available titles average 12.2 million hours viewed per period, compared with 6.1 million for titles available only in some regions. Their share of total viewing moved from 57.2% in 2023H2 to NA in 2026H1. Older library series still matter, though. Titles such as Breaking Bad, Suits, and Money Heist anchor the “steadiest viewership” table, with lowest-period viewing between 70 and 105 million hours. These are reliable floor-setters rather than growth drivers.

Recommendation: Direct most of the 2027 budget to globally available originals, and keep a modest set of library staples that give the platform a dependable baseline of viewing.

2. Hits are rare, and franchises are the safest way to get them

Viewing is top-heavy. The median title earned 1.8 million hours, while the mean was 8.3 million, a gap that shows a small number of titles pulling the average up. In 2026H1, the ten most-watched titles alone captured 6.7% of all viewing time. Even so, no single title took more than about 1.4% of any period, so success is spread across several big releases rather than one runaway hit.

The pattern in those big releases is consistent. Of the seven period leaders, five were later seasons of existing series: Bridgerton Seasons 3 and 4, Squid Game Season 2 (which led two consecutive periods), and Wednesday Season 2. Stranger Things 4 shows the other side of that coin: a very high average, but a standard deviation nearly as large, meaning its viewing came in bursts rather than steadily.

Recommendation: Fund new seasons of proven franchises first, and give the remaining budget to a smaller number of new launches, each with enough marketing support to compete for the top of the chart.

3. Live events should be selective

Live events have grown from 0.0% of viewing hours in 2023H2 to 0.5% in 2026H1. But the typical live entry is modest: it averages 3.6 million hours, against 8.3 million for pre-taped content. The upside comes only at the top. The largest live event reached 235.7 million hours, which exceeds the output of nearly every pre-taped title.

Recommendation: Concentrate live spending on a handful of marquee events with broad appeal instead of expanding the number of events.

Caveats

Netflix rounds hours viewed and omits low-viewing titles, so comparisons among small titles are unreliable. “Views” assumes everyone watches a title in full. The data covers only titles with English-alphabet names, and my shows-versus- movies split is a heuristic. The conclusions above rest on the largest titles, where these limits matter least.
